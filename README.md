# Model Validation Central

Published machine learning methods for molecular property prediction, re-run on the
same molecules, the same folds and the same statistics, so their claims can be read
against one another instead of against whatever each paper chose to compare itself
with.

**[Read the reports →](https://patwalters.github.io/model-validation-central/)**

Most studies here take a published method, or a published claim about a class of
methods, and put it through one fixed evaluation protocol on two unrelated ADME data
sets. The methods differ. The molecules, the splits, the endpoints and the statistics
do not.

One does not, and is marked below. When a paper's claim *is* about the splits, that
protocol cannot test it, because holding the split fixed is the thing being
questioned. Such a study runs on the source paper's own molecules and its own folds
instead, and carries a reproduction of one of that paper's arms as the evidence the
two sets of numbers belong on the same axis. It is comparable within itself and not
with the cards above it.

## What makes these comparable

Every study is scored the same way, and that is the point of collecting them here.

- **The same molecules.** Two collections, 7,608 molecules on nine endpoints from the
  OpenADMET–ExpansionRx blind challenge, and 3,521 on six from Biogen's public ADME
  set. Fifteen endpoints in all.
- **The same splits.** Molecules are clustered with BitBIRCH-Lean, a fixed 30% is held
  out once, and the 25 replicates come from five repeats of a five-fold `GroupKFold`
  over the clusters in what remains. Every method sees identical training molecules in
  every fold and is scored on the same untouched test set, so a difference in the
  metrics is a difference in the method.
- **The same statistics.** Distributions over folds, Tukey HSD corrected for multiple
  comparisons, paired tests using the folds as the pairing, following
  [Even More Thoughts on ML Method Comparisons][thoughts] and the protocol paper it
  points at. There are no bold maxima. Where two methods cannot be separated, both are
  reported as tied.
- **Two data sets, always.** A comparison run once is a hypothesis. The interesting
  question about any result below is which half of it replicates.

Four reference arms — LightGBM on Morgan counts, a single-task D-MPNN, a multitask
D-MPNN, and a D-MPNN initialised from CheMeleon — are fit once, in
`studies/expansion-ml-comparison`, and their per-fold predictions are carried into the
other ADME studies rather than refit, so those cards all sit on one axis. The
cross-study table at the top of the index pools only the studies that carry those
arms; `site/overview.py` refuses anything else, which is why the split-stringency
study does not appear in it.

[thoughts]: https://practicalcheminformatics.blogspot.com/2025/03/even-more-thoughts-on-ml-method.html

### [KERMT v2 and GROVER, fine-tuned on the same folds](https://patwalters.github.io/model-validation-central/reports/kermt.html)

*Does NVIDIA's re-pretrained GROVER encoder, fine-tuned by its own code, hold up against the ADMET field — and how much of any gain survives the fact that it was pre-trained on these molecules?*

KERMT is NVIDIA's GPU-optimised reimplementation of GROVER, a dual-view graph transformer; its released checkpoint, NV-KERMT-70M-v2, re-pretrains that encoder with a contrastive objective. All three checkpoints — v2, GROVER base and GROVER large — are fine-tuned here by KERMT's own main.py finetune, 25 fold models each, and v2's frozen 512-d latent is also read by TabPFN 3 with Monroe's call, against LightGBM, fine-tuned CheMeleon, Monroe + TabPFN 3 and fine-tuned Atom-JEPA.

> **What came out.** Fine-tuned v2 beats fine-tuned GROVER base 30–13–2 over 45 endpoint x metric calls and GROVER large 33–12–0, while GROVER large against base is 3–26–16, so the larger original checkpoint is if anything the weaker one. All three beat LightGBM and fine-tuned CheMeleon, but v2 trails fine-tuned Atom-JEPA 7–11–27 and wins no Biogen call against Monroe + TabPFN 3 (0–2–16), though it is ahead of Monroe on ExpansionRx (15–1–11). No arm fitted here is ever best alone in a Tukey tier. Frozen under TabPFN 3, v2's latent is behind Monroe's on 13 of 15 endpoints and wins 3 of 45 calls; fine-tuning beats it 41–4–0.

**Puts to the test**

- Rong, Y.; Bian, Y.; Xu, T.; Xie, W.; Wei, Y.; Huang, W.; Huang, J. **Self-Supervised Graph Transformer on Large-Scale Molecular Data.** *NeurIPS 2020.* [NeurIPS 2020](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2020/hash/94aef38441efa3380a3bed3faf1f9d5d-Abstract.html)
- Adrian, C.; Mahajan, S. P.; Sirelkhatim, A.; et al. **KERMT: Accelerating Molecular Property Prediction with GPU-Optimised Graph Transformers.** *Preprint, 2025.* [github:NVIDIA-BioNeMo/KERMT](https://github.com/NVIDIA-BioNeMo/KERMT)
- Xue, D.; Mahajan, S. P.; Adrian, C.; et al. **Contrastive Pre-training for Molecular Property Prediction at Scale.** *Preprint, 2026.* [hf:nvidia/NV-KERMT-70M-v2](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/NV-KERMT-70M-v2)

*The released v2 checkpoint was pre-trained on a corpus containing every ExpansionRx and Biogen molecule, test rows included (structures, no labels), so the v2-vs-GROVER gap mixes better pre-training with structural exposure and this design cannot separate them; the two GROVER checkpoints pre-date these data sets and are the unexposed control. KERMT's load_checkpoint also copies the pre-trained checkpoint's saved args over the command line, so v2 trains with dropout 0.1 and selects on validation MAE while both GROVER arms train with no dropout and select on validation loss; the code was run as released and every fold records what it used.*

8 arms · 15 endpoints · 150 fine-tuned fold models · 149 h GPU time

### [Uni-Mol and Uni-Mol2, out of the box](https://patwalters.github.io/model-validation-central/reports/unimol.html)

*Do Uni-Mol and Uni-Mol2, fine-tuned with their own package at its defaults, hold up against the ADMET field?*

Uni-Mol and Uni-Mol2 pre-train 3D transformers on hundreds of millions of conformers, and ship a fine-tuning package, unimol_tools. Both are fine-tuned here with that package's own multitask recipe at its defaults, 25 fold models each, and both encoders are also frozen and read by TabPFN 3 with Monroe's exact call, against LightGBM, fine-tuned CheMeleon, Monroe + TabPFN 3 and fine-tuned Atom-JEPA.

> **What came out.** Fine-tuned Uni-Mol2 beats Uni-Mol 30–12–3 over 45 endpoint × metric calls, fine-tuned CheMeleon 31–8–6 and LightGBM 42–3–0, and reaches the top Tukey tier on 14 of 27 ExpansionRx combinations, never alone. It splits ExpansionRx with Monroe + TabPFN 3 (14–5–8) but loses all 18 Biogen calls, and trails fine-tuned Atom-JEPA 3–6–36. Frozen under TabPFN 3 both Uni-Mol vectors lose all 45 calls to Monroe's; fine-tuning wins 40 and 41 of 45 against them.

**Puts to the test**

- Zhou, G.; Gao, Z.; Ding, Q.; Zheng, H.; Xu, H.; Wei, Z.; Zhang, L.; Ke, G. **Uni-Mol: A Universal 3D Molecular Representation Learning Framework.** *ICLR 2023.* [openreview:6K2RM6wVqKu](https://openreview.net/forum?id=6K2RM6wVqKu)
- Ji, X.; Wang, Z.; Gao, Z.; et al. **Uni-Mol2: Exploring Molecular Pretraining Model at Scale.** *NeurIPS 2024.* [arXiv:2406.14969](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14969)

*The package fits its target scaler on all five training fifths before splitting, so each held-out fifth slightly informs the scaling used for early stopping; the test set is never involved. Uni-Mol2 310M was dropped because it does not fit at batch 16 in 16 GB.*

8 arms · 15 endpoints · 100 fine-tuned fold models · 43 h GPU time

### [Counterfactuals from one analog](https://patwalters.github.io/model-validation-central/reports/causal-chemprop.html)

*Does a structural causal model on Chemprop's embedding, anchored on one measured member of a series, rank the rest of the series better?*

Causal-Chemprop fits a linear SCM, learned by DAGMA, over Chemprop's last hidden layer and the target, and predicts a scaffold series as counterfactuals of one measured anchor. It is run with the authors' code on the paper's eleven kinase tasks, released and refitted, and per fold and per series on ExpansionRx and Biogen, against its own encoder, Monroe + TabPFN 3 with and without the anchors, fine-tuned Atom-JEPA, CheMeleon and LightGBM, each also given the anchor as a residual offset.

> **What came out.** The released checkpoints reproduce Table 1 in 65 of 66 values and beat the released Chemprop on 9 of 11 tasks; refitting encoder and SCM loses ρ on 10 of 11 and gains on none. On the 182 ExpansionRx and 22 Biogen series its within-series ρ is behind its own encoder on 152 and 20 and behind Monroe + TabPFN 3 on 159 and 22. The counterfactual is what loses the ranking: series predicted entirely by it reach ρ −0.02 and 0.12 against the encoder's 0.65 and 0.69.

**Puts to the test**

- Natajaya, C.; Attia, L.; Burns, J. W.; Doyle, P. S. **Causal-Chemprop: Causal Machine Learning for Molecular Property Prediction and Design.** *J. Chem. Inf. Model. 2026.* [10.1021/acs.jcim.6c01429](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jcim.6c01429)

11 paper tasks · 204 ADME series · 1,020 SCM fits · 7 arms per series  
Code: [`studies/causal-chemprop`](studies/causal-chemprop/) · Report builder: `11_build_page.py`

### [Pre-training in 3D on quantum mechanics](https://patwalters.github.io/model-validation-central/reports/quadmet-former.html)

*Does pre-training a 3D transformer on quantum-mechanical properties help ADMET prediction?*

QuADMET-Former pre-trains a 1.3M-parameter TorchMD-ET equivariant transformer on PubChemQC quantum properties — total energy, HOMO–LUMO gap, dipole moment, E3FP fingerprints and Löwdin charges — across ten released checkpoints, then adapts it by linear probing followed by fine-tuning. Both a fine-tuned arm and its frozen embedding read by TabPFN 3 are run on the authors' code and weights, against LightGBM, fine-tuned CheMeleon, Monroe + TabPFN 3 and fine-tuned Atom-JEPA.

> **What came out.** Pre-trained LP-FT beats from-scratch training on 14 of 15 endpoints by a median +0.083 R², and LightGBM on 38 of 45 combinations. Against the pre-trained field it reaches the top Tukey tier on 5 of 27 ExpansionRx combinations, nearly splits with fine-tuned CheMeleon (15 wins, 9 ties, 21 losses over both data sets), and trails fine-tuned Atom-JEPA (2 wins, 35 losses) and Monroe + TabPFN 3 (10 wins, 33 losses).

**Puts to the test**

- Raja, A.; et al. **QuADMET-Former: Pre-training on Quantum Mechanical Properties Improves ADMET Prediction.** *ChemRxiv, 2026.* [doi:10.26434/chemrxiv.15002429/v3](https://doi.org/10.26434/chemrxiv.15002429/v3)

*Six discrepancies between the paper and the authors' repository were found and are documented in the study README; the arms here use the released checkpoints with those issues worked around.*

6 methods · 15 endpoints · 10 released QM checkpoints · 1.2 h GPU time

### [Fine-tuning in 3D](https://patwalters.github.io/model-validation-central/reports/atom-jepa.html)

*Does a 3D equivariant encoder pre-trained on 19 million conformers beat the best frozen encoder?*

Atom-JEPA pre-trains a 39M-parameter EquiformerV3 by latent prediction on 19M Uni-Mol conformers and fine-tunes it, multitask, on ten conformers per molecule. It is run here with the authors' own code and released weights, against the arm the paper did not run, Monroe + TabPFN 3, and a frozen Atom-JEPA arm read by the same TabPFN 3 call separates the representation from the fine-tuning.

> **What came out.** Fine-tuned Atom-JEPA is ahead of fine-tuned CheMeleon on 38 of 45 combinations and of LightGBM on all 45. Against Monroe it is ahead on 6 of 9 ExpansionRx endpoints and behind on 4 of 6 Biogen ones, and training-set size does not explain the split (ρ = −0.04). Frozen, its embedding never beats Monroe's, a median −0.09 R²; fine-tuning never loses to freezing and adds +0.13 on ExpansionRx and +0.07 on Biogen.

**Puts to the test**

- Petersen, K. H.; Tirsgaard, R. H.; Cornet, F. R. J.; Jordahn, M.; Schmidt, M. N. **Atom-JEPA: Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture for 3D Atomistic Systems.** *Preprint, 2026.* [arXiv:2610.08400](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08400)

### [Borrowing errors](https://patwalters.github.io/model-validation-central/reports/molpair.html)

*Does correcting each prediction with the known errors of similar training molecules help?*

MolPAIR corrects one tabular foundation model with another: a global TabPFN 3 reads a frozen CheMeleon fingerprint, and a second TabPFN 3 learns from pairs of training molecules how the first model's error changes between similar molecules, so each test molecule borrows the errors of up to 32 neighbours. It is put beside LightGBM, fine-tuned CheMeleon and Monroe + TabPFN 3.

> **What came out.** Against the prediction it corrects, MolPAIR is significantly ahead on 41 of 45 endpoint × metric combinations and behind on 2, and improves 51–66% of individual predictions, close to the paper's 55–57%. The gain holds, at half the size, on the test molecules the paper's own split would have kept. But the correction is worth +0.024 R², fine-tuning CheMeleon +0.047 and Monroe's embedding +0.129: MolPAIR loses to fine-tuned CheMeleon on 30 combinations and to Monroe + TabPFN 3 on 34.

**Puts to the test**

- Lee, J.; Lee, D.; Jeong, M.; Yoo, J. **Molecular Property Prediction under Structural Shift with Tabular Foundation Models.** *Preprint, 2026.* [arXiv:2609.38744](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38744)

*A reimplementation. The paper's code link does not resolve, so this is written from its Section 3 and Appendix A.*

6 arms · 15 endpoints · 375 fold models · 41/45 ahead of its baseline

### [Swapping the head](https://patwalters.github.io/model-validation-central/reports/sdm-tabular.html)

*Holding Monroe's frozen embedding fixed, which in-context tabular model reads it best?*

Every tabular foundation model in NVIDIA's structured-data-models package — TabICLv2, Kumo Tabular small, medium and large, and the 1.6-billion-parameter TabFM — reads the same frozen 720-d Monroe embedding on the same folds as Monroe + TabPFN 3, so the only thing that differs between arms is the head. The authors' own tabicl package, loading the same checkpoint as NVIDIA's TabICLv2 port, is the control.

> **What came out.** Kumo Tabular large is on top of all 45 endpoint × metric combinations after Tukey's correction, the only arm never significantly worse than the best. In paired tests it is ahead of TabPFN 3 on 28 and behind on 6, and ahead of TabFM on 28 against 7 for an eighth of the GPU time. NVIDIA's TabICLv2 port reproduces upstream tabicl to within 0.008 R². Swapping heads moves R² by a median of 0.042 per endpoint, against 0.208 for the representation.

**Puts to the test**

- Qu, J.; et al. **TabICLv2: A Better, Faster, Scalable, and Open Tabular Foundation Model.** *ICML 2026.* [arXiv:2602.11139](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11139)

### [What survives a hard split](https://patwalters.github.io/model-validation-central/reports/split-fragility.html)

*A paper says nearest-neighbour lookup matches modern ML under every split, and that all of them collapse to a naive baseline out of distribution. Does that hold against models it did not have?*

Eleven arms on the ten ChEMBL potency sets and six splitting schemes of Lee, Moldagulov and Grzybowski, run on that paper's own folds: four methods fitted here, the paper's naive baseline, and the paper's own six arms imported at their published per-fold numbers. 1,500 fold models, and a re-implementation of the paper's k-NN that reproduces 250 of 250 of its released folds to 4e-9, which is what licenses putting the two sources on one axis.

> **What came out.** Half the paper replicates. Error does climb as the splits harden, but so does the naive baseline it is measured against, and reading the gap instead of the raw MAE splits the two claims apart. k-NN is at the top of none of the 18 scheme x metric combinations and Monroe's frozen encoder is at the top of all 18, separating from k-NN on all six schemes and on five of six under a conservative target-level test. On the hardest schemes k-NN takes 0.005 and 0.010 pIC50 off the naive predictor and the paper's own GNN goes negative, while Monroe holds 0.121 and 0.141. The decay rates are nearly identical across arms, 0.81 to 0.98; what differs is the offset, so nothing here is more robust to distance, it is just uniformly better.

**Puts to the test**

- Lee, K.; Moldagulov, G.; Grzybowski, B. A. **The Fragility of Bioactivity Prediction: Rigorous Dataset Splits Expose the Illusion of ML Accuracy.** *Chem. Eur. J. 2026, e71208.* [doi:10.1002/chem.71208](https://doi.org/10.1002/chem.71208)
- Janela, T.; Bajorath, J. **Simple nearest-neighbour analysis meets the accuracy of compound potency predictions using complex machine learning models.** *Nat. Mach. Intell. 2022, 4, 1246-1255.* [doi:10.1038/s42256-022-00581-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-022-00581-6)

*The paper's six arms are its own published per-fold metrics, not re-run, and each had 50 Optuna trials per fold where the four arms fitted here use library defaults. The upstream release ships two disagreeing copies of the splits; the .pkl copy is used, because it reproduces the paper's numbers and the .json copy does not.*

11 arms · 6 splitting schemes · 1,500 fold models fitted · 250/250 published folds reproduced · 0/18 combinations k-NN leads

### [Nine ways to model ADME](https://patwalters.github.io/model-validation-central/reports/expansion-ml-comparison.html)

*Which foundation model, and does it replicate?*

Nine modelling approaches over fifteen ADME and physicochemical endpoints on two unrelated data sets, 25 replicate models each, every one scored on a held-out test set it never saw. Four of the nine are pre-trained molecular foundation models, and only one of those four wins anything at all. The one that wins does no downstream training whatsoever — it freezes a pre-trained encoder and predicts each endpoint in context — and it appears three times, under three different tabular models, which is what lets the page price the representation against the predictor bolted onto it.

> **What came out.** Monroe's frozen encoder is at the top of 21 of 27 ExpansionRx combinations and all 18 Biogen ones; MEGA-CL and Mol-JEPA are best on nothing, anywhere. Crossing two frozen representations with two in-context heads settles which half does the work: changing the head moves R² by a median of 0.016 and not even in a consistent direction, while changing the representation moves it by 0.168 on fifteen endpoints out of fifteen. The representation is worth about ten times the head.

**Puts to the test**

- Banaszewski, B.; Fitzgibbon, A. W. **Monroe: A Molecular Foundation Model for In-Context Probabilistic Inference.** *Preprint, 2026.* [arXiv:2608.18982](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18982)

### [Pre-training on fingerprints](https://patwalters.github.io/model-validation-central/reports/ecfp-pretrain.html)

*A graph network taught to predict fingerprints, against the fingerprints*

PT-GIN is a Graph Isomorphism Network pre-trained on 462,189 QMugs molecules to predict each molecule's own 2,048-bit hashed ECFP4, then frozen and handed to LightGBM. Its authors report it beating hashed ECFP on five of six Biogen ADME tasks. Here it is run against those same fingerprints through that same predictor, so the only thing that differs between the two arms is the representation.

> **What came out.** It wins six endpoints of fifteen and loses six; on Biogen, its own benchmark, it wins one and loses three. Which six it wins is not about how much data an endpoint has — it is about how well the fingerprints were already doing. PT-GIN helps where the baseline was weak and hurts where it was strong, Spearman ρ = −0.65 with the baseline R².

**Puts to the test**

- Money-Kyrle, S.; Dablander, M.; Hanser, T.; Werner, S.; Deane, C. M.; Morris, G. M. **On Improving Graph Neural Networks for QSAR by Pre-training on Extended-Connectivity Fingerprints.** *Preprint, 2026.* [arXiv:2605.10722](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10722)

### [Does multimodal fusion help?](https://patwalters.github.io/model-validation-central/reports/multimodal-fusion.html)

*Unimodal versus multimodal, on ADME data*

Thirty-three ways of combining four molecular representations — RDKit descriptors, Mol2Vec, a supervised GNN's graph embedding and a character BiGRU's SMILES embedding — crossed with early and late fusion and three final learners, over the same fifteen endpoints. 12,375 fitted configurations and 6,375 more for the two controls.

> **What came out.** The paper's central finding replicates: fusing modalities moves accuracy very little, and none of it survives a correction for multiple comparisons. What fusion buys is calibration. Two things the paper could not see from inside its own design also show up — a single well-initialised graph network beats all thirty-three configurations on both data sets, and when the GNN modality is actually a learned representation, which in the released code it is not, the ranking of which modality matters inverts.

**Puts to the test**

- Wasswa, J.; Kajjumba, G. W.; Ramsundar, B. **Unimodal vs Multimodal Learning: A Systematic Evaluation of Fusion Strategies and Model Design for Molecular Property Prediction and Uncertainty Quantification.** *J. Chem. Inf. Model. 2026.* [doi:10.1021/acs.jcim.6c01878](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jcim.6c01878)

*A reimplementation. The authors' MIT-licensed release is a set of Colab dumps that cannot be run, so this is written from that source read as a specification alongside the paper's Supporting Information.*

### [Sixty models per fold](https://patwalters.github.io/model-validation-central/reports/trimole-hybrid.html)

*Is picking a model per endpoint worth it?*

Trimole-Hybrid is not a model but a selection procedure. For every endpoint and every fold it builds sixty candidate predictors — five molecular views crossed with three blocks of chemistry priors crossed with four backends — fits all of them, scores them on the held-out validation fifth, and keeps exactly one to predict the test set. It is put against four fixed architectures on the same fifteen endpoints.

> **What came out.** It loses to a single well-initialised graph network on both data sets, for two reasons only visible in what it selected: nothing in its pool learns a representation, and on ExpansionRx the split it selects on does not resemble the split it is scored on. What it chose does not replicate either.

**Puts to the test**

- Luo, Z.; Huang, D.; Shao, Y.; Yu, Q.; Li, Y. **A Multimodal Representation Learning Platform for Accurate Molecular ADMET Prediction.** *Bioinformatics 2026, in review.* [doi:10.1101/2026.08.24.746660](https://doi.org/10.1101/2026.08.24.746660)

*A reimplementation. The authors' release describes itself as not runnable, so this reproduces the method, not the paper's numbers.*

5 methods · 60 candidates per fold · 22k candidate fits · 1,425 fold models  
Code: [`studies/expansion-ml-comparison`](studies/expansion-ml-comparison/) · Report builder: `14_build_trimole_page.py`

## Layout

```
studies/<slug>/          one self-contained study: scripts, README, requirements,
                         per-fold metrics, tables, figures, and a study.json
site/build_site.py       reads every study.json, writes docs/
site/overview.py         the cross-study Tukey comparison at the top of the index
site/style.py            the stylesheet the index shares with the reports
docs/                    the GitHub Pages site
docs/index.html            the overall report, one card per study report
docs/reports/<slug>.html   each study's own report, copied from its results/
docs/overview.json         the tally table, and docs/assets/ the figures with it
```

A study directory stands on its own. It carries its own `config.py`,
`requirements.txt`, README and results, and it is run from inside itself, so it works
exactly as it did before it was collected here and can be lifted back out.

## Rebuilding

The index needs nothing but a Python interpreter:

```bash
python site/build_site.py            # rebuild docs/ and the card block above
python site/build_site.py --check    # say what is stale, change nothing
```

That copies each study's built report into `docs/reports/` and regenerates the index
and the card block in this README from the `study.json` files. It does not run any
science. A study's report is built inside that study's own directory, in the
environment that study needs — see its README for the commands and its
`requirements.txt` for the environment.

The cross-study comparison at the top of the index is the one part that does read
the numbers, so it is a separate script and needs pandas, statsmodels and
matplotlib:

```bash
python site/overview.py              # -> docs/overview.json, docs/assets/*.png
python site/build_site.py            # embeds what it wrote
```

The table and the figures use the same metric, so a row and its panel can never
disagree about who won: one Tukey HSD per endpoint, counted best alone / tied /
worse. That metric is MAE by default, since it is in the units of the endpoint;
`--metric r2` or `--metric spearman` rebuilds both against either of the others.

Run it when a study's `fold_metrics.csv` changes. It refuses to pool anything
unless the four reference arms are identical across the studies that carry them,
and it asserts the tie rule on every endpoint × metric combination before
counting.

## Adding a study

1. Drop the study directory under `studies/<slug>/`. It keeps its own README,
   `requirements.txt` and results.
2. Add a `studies/<slug>/study.json`:

   ```json
   {
     "slug": "my-study",
     "name": "my-study",
     "datasets": ["ExpansionRx", "Biogen ADME"],
     "endpoints": 15,
     "protocol": "5 x 5 GroupKFold over chemical clusters, one fixed holdout, Tukey HSD",
     "updated": "2026-09-02",
     "reports": [
       {
         "slug": "my-study",
         "date": "2026-09-02",
         "title": "The title on the card and the report",
         "eyebrow": "What kind of question this is",
         "question": "The one-line question the study answers",
         "summary": "Two or three sentences on what was compared and how much of it.",
         "finding": "What came out, in one or two sentences, including the number that matters.",
         "facts": [{"n": "15", "label": "endpoints"}],
         "validates": [
           {
             "name": "Short name for the method",
             "authors": "Author, A.; Author, B.; et al.",
             "title": "The paper's full title",
             "venue": "J. Chem. Inf. Model. 2026",
             "label": "doi:10.1021/...",
             "url": "https://doi.org/..."
           }
         ],
         "caveat": "Optional. Say so here if this is a reimplementation.",
         "source": "results/report.html",
         "builder": "06_build_page.py"
       }
     ]
   }
   ```

   `slug`, `title`, `question`, `summary`, `finding` and `source` are required;
   everything else is optional. A study with two reports — as
   `expansion-ml-comparison` has — lists both, and gets a card for each.

   Cards are ordered newest first, by each report's `date`, so a new study lands
   at the top on its own and nothing has to be renumbered. A report without a
   `date` falls back to its study's `updated`, and one with neither sorts last.
   Reports built on the same day are separated by their study's slug, which only
   keeps the order stable and means nothing.

   Each entry in `validates` is a paper the study puts to the test, cited in
   full on the card: `title`, `authors` and `venue` are printed, `label` is the
   link text and `url` is where it points.
3. Run `python site/build_site.py`.

If the new study reuses the reference arms, point its importer at
`studies/expansion-ml-comparison`; `studies/ecfp-pretrain/00_import_baselines.py` is
the worked example.
